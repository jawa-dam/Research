# GEI Founding Researcher Recruitment Plan

**Campaign:** GEI — Put It to the Test  
**Initial goal:** Invite one founding independent reviewer in each of seven specialist pathways.  
**Campaign duration:** 30 days from launch.  
**Public message:** *You bring the expertise. We'll bring the blueprint.*  
**Research principle:** *Challenge the blueprint. Agreement is not required.*

---

## 1. Campaign purpose

The first recruitment campaign is designed to build a small, credible group of independent contributors who can examine clearly bounded questions within Genesis Engineered Interpretation (GEI).

GEI proposes comparisons between selected features of Genesis 1–6 and water-management, structural, mechanical, and ancient-technology concepts. These comparisons are hypotheses for investigation—not established translations, archaeological discoveries, historical conclusions, or academic consensus.

The campaign is successful when it produces careful questions, documented evidence, useful corrections, and testable next steps. Agreement with GEI is not a recruitment requirement.

### First 30-day target

- Identify approximately three potential contacts for each of the seven pathways (about 21 prospects).
- Send a small, tailored first wave of invitations rather than mass unsolicited messages.
- Aim for at least one substantive conversation in each pathway.
- Invite suitable contributors to review one bounded research question before proposing a large interdisciplinary commitment.
- Record objections and negative findings with the same care as supportive observations.

These are planning targets, not promised outcomes. A smaller group of responsive, relevant reviewers is better than a larger list of unqualified names.

## 2. Seven founding reviewer pathways

### 1) Hebrew linguistics — Examine the Hebrew

**Best-fit expertise:** Biblical Hebrew, Northwest Semitic linguistics, historical linguistics, lexicography, morphology, syntax, and semantics.

**First question:** What do the relevant Hebrew forms and their contexts actually support, and where does the proposed interpretation exceed the linguistic evidence?

**Initial review scope:**
- Examine the Hebrew text, morphology, syntax, semantic range, and contextual usage.
- Distinguish Hebrew etymology from English sound associations, including the proposed Adam / “A-DAM” wordplay.
- Identify relevant lexicons, grammars, textual variants, and competing interpretations.
- State clearly what cannot be established from the language alone.

**Invitation line:** “Would you be willing to examine a small set of Hebrew-language claims and identify what the text supports, what it does not support, and what needs correction?”

### 2) Biblical and textual studies — Examine the text

**Best-fit expertise:** Hebrew Bible / Old Testament, biblical literature, textual criticism, translation studies, literary structure, and ancient interpretation.

**First question:** Does the proposed pattern follow the text, sequence, genre, and literary context of Genesis 1–6, or does it depend on selective reading?

**Initial review scope:**
- Examine the selected passages in context and compare relevant translations.
- Assess sequence, repetition, literary structure, genre, and interpretive assumptions.
- Identify the strongest conventional and alternative readings.
- Distinguish textual observation from theological or engineering inference.

**Invitation line:** “I’m looking for an independent reading of a proposed pattern in Genesis 1–6, especially its sequence, literary context, and strongest alternative explanations.”

### 3) Civil engineering — Test the structure

**Best-fit expertise:** Civil, structural, geotechnical, or water-resources engineering; dams, foundations, retaining structures, reservoirs, channels, and hydraulic works.

**First question:** Are the proposed containment and structural relationships physically coherent under clearly stated assumptions?

**Initial review scope:**
- Define the physical system being proposed before evaluating it.
- Identify required geometry, materials, foundation conditions, loads, seepage controls, outlets, and failure modes.
- Separate a useful modern analogy from evidence of an ancient engineered structure.
- State what measurements or design details are missing.

**Invitation line:** “Could you stress-test the structural side of a proposed Genesis-to-water-management analogy and tell us what would be physically plausible, implausible, or underspecified?”

### 4) Hydrology — Investigate the flow

**Best-fit expertise:** Hydrology, watershed science, hydraulics, river systems, groundwater, water resources, and fluid mechanics.

**First question:** Do the proposed water sources, elevations, storage, separation, drainage, and release sequences form a coherent hydrological model?

**Initial review scope:**
- Map the proposed water balance, watershed, elevation/head, storage, inflow, outflow, and drainage assumptions.
- Identify whether the sequence requires processes that are inconsistent or unsupported.
- Distinguish metaphorical flow language from a quantitatively testable model.
- Recommend the minimum data needed for a meaningful test.

**Invitation line:** “Would you review the water-flow assumptions in this proposed model and identify what can be tested hydrologically—and what cannot yet be inferred?”

### 5) Archaeology — Examine the ancient evidence

**Best-fit expertise:** Archaeology, archaeometry, landscape archaeology, ancient water systems, irrigation, canals, dams, reservoirs, mills, and material culture of the ancient Near East.

**First question:** What securely dated archaeological evidence is relevant to the proposed technologies, and what do chronology, geography, and context allow us to say?

**Initial review scope:**
- Identify securely dated examples of ancient water-control and milling technologies.
- Assess chronology, location, construction evidence, use, and cultural context.
- Identify gaps between the proposed model and known material evidence.
- Avoid treating resemblance as proof of historical connection.

**Invitation line:** “I’m seeking an archaeological reality check on the technologies being compared—not confirmation that a specific biblical structure existed.”

### 6) Mechanical engineering — Test the mechanism

**Best-fit expertise:** Mechanical engineering, fluid machinery, power transmission, machine design, waterwheels, mills, and historical mechanical technology.

**First question:** Could the proposed waterwheel or mill mechanism operate under the flow, head, torque, and power assumptions being suggested?

**Initial review scope:**
- Define the proposed machine and its operating conditions.
- Examine flow rate, head, wheel type, torque, power, gearing, shafting, and material constraints.
- Identify missing components or incompatible assumptions.
- Explain what evidence would be required to move from analogy to a credible mechanical reconstruction.

**Invitation line:** “Could you test whether the proposed waterwheel/mill comparison works mechanically, using explicit assumptions rather than visual resemblance?”

### 7) Ancient Near Eastern studies — Compare the traditions

**Best-fit expertise:** Assyriology, Sumerology, Akkadian, Sumerian, Egyptology, ancient Near Eastern religions, comparative literature, and ancient creation traditions.

**First question:** How do relevant ancient creation accounts compare in their original languages, historical settings, and literary structures—and where do important differences undermine a proposed comparison?

**Initial review scope:**
- Identify relevant primary texts and reliable translations.
- Compare motifs, vocabulary, literary structures, and historical contexts.
- Distinguish direct textual relationships from broad thematic parallels.
- Flag anachronisms, overgeneralizations, and unsupported claims of influence.

**Invitation line:** “Would you help evaluate the ancient Near Eastern context and compare relevant creation traditions without assuming that similarity establishes dependence or a shared engineering blueprint?”

## 3. Who to approach and how to identify candidates

The goal is to find people whose published work, teaching, professional practice, or research interests directly match a narrow question.

### Discovery venues

Use professional societies, university department directories, conference programs, research publications, institutional repositories, and specialist research groups as places to identify relevant expertise. Examples include:

- Society of Biblical Literature (SBL): https://www.sbl-site.org/
- American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE): https://www.asce.org/
- American Geophysical Union (AGU): https://www.agu.org/
- American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME): https://www.asme.org/
- Archaeological Institute of America (AIA): https://www.archaeological.org/

For Ancient Near Eastern studies, search university departments, research centers, conference programs, and publications focused on Assyriology, Sumerian and Akkadian studies, Egyptology, and ancient literature.

These organizations and venues are discovery resources only. Their inclusion does not imply endorsement, partnership, or affiliation with GEI.

### Candidate fit checklist

Prioritize candidates who:
- Have demonstrable expertise in the specific question being offered.
- Have relevant publications, projects, teaching, or professional experience.
- Can explain methods and evidence clearly.
- Are open to independent evaluation, including criticism.
- Have no apparent conflict that would prevent an independent assessment.
- Can offer a bounded contribution within a reasonable time.

Do not select candidates merely because they are prominent, likely to agree, or have a large social-media following. Do not imply that a person has accepted, endorsed, or reviewed GEI until they explicitly do so.

### Prospect research record

Keep a private outreach tracker with these fields:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Prospect ID | Internal code, e.g. HEB-01 or CIV-02 |
| Discipline | One of the seven pathways |
| Name | Public professional name |
| Public profile | Institutional or professional page |
| Relevant expertise | Specific topic supported by public evidence |
| Reason for fit | One or two sentences tied to the proposed review question |
| Contact route | Publicly listed professional email or official contact form |
| Date identified | Date prospect was added |
| Outreach status | See campaign stages below |
| Last contact / next follow-up | Dates |
| Response summary | Factual, respectful summary |
| Consent / attribution | Record only what the person explicitly agrees to |

Keep private email addresses, private correspondence, and application details out of public GitHub issues, pull requests, commits, and static website files. Do not scrape or bulk-message people. Use contact information they have made available for professional inquiries and respect any request not to be contacted again.

## 4. Outreach sequence and message templates

### Outreach principles

- Personalize the opening with a genuine reference to the person's relevant work.
- Ask for one bounded review question, not blanket approval of GEI.
- Be transparent that GEI is an independent research hypothesis.
- State that disagreement and correction are welcome.
- Keep the first message concise; attach or link only the most relevant public material.
- Do not claim that other experts have joined unless they have.
- Do not imply institutional sponsorship, formal peer review, compensation, or a funded position.
- Send one courteous follow-up after approximately 7–10 days; if there is no reply, close the outreach unless the person invites further contact.

### Core introduction

Use this as the common foundation, adapting it to each recipient:

> I’m developing an independent research framework called Genesis Engineered Interpretation (GEI). It examines whether selected features of Genesis 1–6 can be meaningfully compared with water-management and engineering concepts. I am not asking you to accept the framework or endorse its conclusions. I’m seeking a specialist to examine one clearly defined question, identify weaknesses, and point us toward the evidence or tests that would be needed. A critical or negative assessment would be valuable.

Would you be open to reviewing a small, clearly bounded set of Hebrew-language observations and identifying what the morphology, syntax, semantic range, and context support—or rule out?

## 5. Thirty-day execution calendar

### Days 1–7 — Prepare a credible first approach

- Confirm the public recruitment page and participation guide are live and accurate.
- Review all seven pathway descriptions for scope and terminology.
- Prepare a one-page research brief for each pathway, with one question, relevant source material, current assumptions, known limitations, and requested review.
- Create a private outreach tracker; do not place personal contact details in the public repository.
- Prepare a short project introduction and a direct link to the public research page.
- Confirm what participation can currently mean. Do not promise a private portal, account, application workflow, paid role, formal peer review, or institutional affiliation unless actually implemented.

**Deliverable:** Seven concise specialist briefs and a private prospect tracker.

### Days 8–14 — Identify a first prospect pool

- Identify approximately three suitable prospects per pathway.
- Use publications, institutional profiles, professional directories, and conference programs to establish fit.
- Record why each person is relevant using publicly available professional information.
- Prefer direct expertise over celebrity or general visibility.
- Check that the invitation question is within the prospect's stated expertise.

**Deliverable:** A prioritized list of up to 21 prospects, with a documented reason for each selection.

### Days 15–21 — Send tailored invitations

- Begin with a small first wave, ideally one or two carefully selected contacts per pathway.
- Personalize every message; do not send a generic bulk blast.
- Include one specific question and one public project link.
- Record the date, channel, and status.
- Respond promptly and respectfully to questions or corrections.
- Do not publicly name recipients or imply participation without permission.

**Deliverable:** First-wave invitations sent and responses tracked privately.

### Days 22–30 — Hold conversations and define first reviews

- Follow up once after 7–10 days where appropriate.
- Offer a short introductory conversation or a written review of one bounded question.
- Agree on the scope, expected output, timeline, attribution preference, and whether the contribution may be made public.
- Capture objections and source recommendations accurately.
- Identify possible roundtable participants only after individual scope and interest are clear.

**Deliverable:** Initial reviewer conversations, defined next steps, and a documented list of open research questions.

## 6. Outreach tracking stages

Use these statuses consistently:

1. **Identified** — candidate found; fit not yet fully assessed.
2. **Qualified** — public evidence supports relevant expertise.
3. **Prioritized** — selected for a specific first-wave invitation.
4. **Contacted** — tailored invitation sent.
5. **Follow-up due** — one follow-up is appropriate after 7–10 days.
6. **Responded** — any reply received; record the response accurately.
7. **Conversation scheduled** — introductory discussion agreed.
8. **Review proposed** — bounded review question and scope shared.
9. **Contributing** — person explicitly agreed to a defined contribution.
10. **Paused / declined / no response** — close respectfully; honor requests not to be contacted again.

Do not treat a positive reply as agreement to review, a review as endorsement, or a contribution as approval of the entire GEI framework.

## 7. Application and reviewer selection standards

Use the existing [Researcher Participation Guide](./RESEARCHER-PARTICIPATION-GUIDE.md) for participation types, application states, and research integrity principles.

### First-contribution selection criteria

Assess each proposed contribution on:
- **Relevant expertise:** Does the person's background fit the specific question?
- **Method clarity:** Are the methods and sources identifiable?
- **Scope discipline:** Does the contribution stay within its discipline?
- **Evidence quality:** Are claims supported by sources, data, or transparent reasoning?
- **Critical independence:** Are limitations and alternative explanations considered?
- **Practical value:** Does the review identify a useful correction, test, or next step?

Credentials can help establish fit but should not replace evaluation of the contribution itself. Independent researchers and students may contribute when their work is relevant and transparent.

### Suggested first review format

Ask each reviewer to provide, if willing:

1. The question reviewed.
2. What is accurate or supportable.
3. What is inaccurate, unsupported, or overstated.
4. Relevant sources or data.
5. Strongest alternative explanation.
6. What evidence would change the assessment.
7. Recommended next test.
8. Whether the contributor consents to public attribution and publication.

Attribution and publication permission must be explicit and separate. Do not publish private correspondence or identify a reviewer without consent.

## 8. Roundtable selection and format

Do not convene a roundtable simply to display a list of disciplines. Convene it when participants have reviewed bounded questions and there is a substantive shared problem to discuss.

### Selection criteria

- Relevant expertise for the specific agenda.
- Willingness to discuss limitations and disagreement respectfully.
- A defined contribution or question already reviewed.
- Clear consent regarding participation, recording, notes, and attribution.
- No implication that attendance equals endorsement.

### Suggested first meeting — 80 minutes

| Time | Topic |
|---:|---|
| 10 min | Purpose, scope, and central question |
| 10 min | Language and textual observations |
| 15 min | Hydraulic and structural model |
| 15 min | Archaeological and mechanical comparisons |
| 15 min | Objections and alternative explanations |
| 10 min | Tests, missing evidence, and next steps |
| 5 min | Actions and owners |

Open with: **“What would have to be true for this interpretation to be wrong?”**

Record disagreement and unresolved questions. A roundtable is a discussion, not a vote on whether GEI is true.

## 9. Campaign measures

At day 30, review:
- Number of prospects identified and qualified in each pathway.
- Number of tailored invitations sent.
- Response rate and substantive conversations.
- Number of bounded reviews proposed and accepted.
- Number of useful corrections, sources, counterarguments, and testable next steps.
- Disciplines with no suitable prospects or no response.
- Whether any campaign language overstated the project's status.

Do not use agreement rate, number of endorsements, or public names as the primary measure of research quality.

## 10. Risks and safeguards

| Risk | Safeguard |
|---|---|
| Appearing to seek validation | Ask for a narrow independent test; state that disagreement is welcome. |
| Overstating expertise or consensus | Describe GEI as a hypothesis under investigation; report each review's actual scope. |
| Confusing English wordplay with Hebrew etymology | Explicitly separate sound association from source-language claims. |
| Treating analogy as historical evidence | Require dated material evidence and contextual analysis for historical claims. |
| Sending generic or excessive outreach | Personalize each message; limit follow-up to one unless invited. |
| Exposing personal information | Keep contact records and correspondence private; never publish them in GitHub. |
| Implying institutional endorsement | State that discovery venues are not sponsors or partners. |
| Promising unavailable infrastructure | Clearly disclose that private intake and researcher accounts are not live until implemented and tested. |
