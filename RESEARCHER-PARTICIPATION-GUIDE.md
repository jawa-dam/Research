# GEI Researcher Participation Guide

## Purpose

The GEI Open Research Initiative invites independent examination of a proposed interpretive framework comparing selected features of Genesis 1–6 with language, textual structure, water management, engineering, and ancient technologies.

GEI is a research hypothesis under investigation. It is not presented as academic consensus, an established Hebrew translation, proven ancient hydraulic technology, or a confirmed archaeological discovery.

**Challenge the blueprint. Agreement is not required.**

## Seven specialist pathways

| Pathway | Invitation | Initial research question |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew linguistics | Examine the Hebrew | What do the relevant Hebrew words, morphology, syntax, semantic ranges, and contexts actually support? Distinguish Hebrew etymology from English sound associations such as Adam/A-DAM. |
| Biblical and textual studies | Examine the text | Does the proposed mapping follow the structure, genre, sequence, translation history, and context of Genesis 1–6? What are the strongest alternative readings? |
| Civil engineering | Test the structure | Are the proposed containment, foundation, reservoir, outlet, channel, load, and material assumptions physically coherent? |
| Hydrology | Investigate the flow | Are the proposed watershed, elevation, storage, drainage, water balance, and controlled-release assumptions hydrologically plausible? |
| Archaeology | Examine the ancient evidence | What securely dated evidence exists for relevant ancient dams, canals, reservoirs, irrigation, and milling technologies? What chronology and geography constrain comparisons? |
| Mechanical engineering | Test the mechanism | Could the proposed waterwheel or mill operate under the stated flow, head, power-transfer, and mechanical conditions? |
| Ancient Near Eastern studies | Compare the traditions | What do relevant Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and other ancient creation traditions say in their original languages and historical contexts? Which similarities are meaningful, and which differences matter? |

## Participation types

### Research contributors
For scholars, professionals, students, and independent researchers who wish to provide a defined specialist contribution.

### Research supporters
For readers and interested participants who wish to submit questions, source leads, corrections, or constructive critiques, or follow public updates.

Credentials and institutional affiliation may be provided voluntarily. Initial expressions of interest should not require proof of credentials.

## Application information

A future private application intake should request only what is necessary:

- Name or preferred display name
- Private contact email
- Primary discipline and optional secondary discipline
- Optional title, affiliation, ORCID, or professional profile
- Relevant experience and research interests
- Preferred participation type
- A question, concern, or proposed contribution
- Contact consent
- Separate optional public-attribution consent, unchecked by default

Do not request a home address, date of birth, phone number, government identification, or other unnecessary personal information.

Private contact details and private submissions must never be placed in public GitHub issues or pull requests. Public attribution requires explicit opt-in.

## Application lifecycle

Applications use these states:

1. Received
2. Under review
3. Accepted
4. Waitlisted
5. Declined
6. Withdrawn

Application status is separate from the status of an individual research submission.

## Research submission types

Contributors may submit:

- Research question
- Critique or counterargument
- Technical analysis
- Source reference
- Correction
- Collaboration offer

Suggested submission lifecycle:

1. Draft
2. Submitted
3. Triage
4. Under review
5. Response requested
6. Published
7. Not published
8. Withdrawn

A submission may be declined for publication without implying that the contributor's view is invalid. The reason and review outcome should be recorded respectfully.

## Shared review template

Every research contribution should include:

1. **Research ID and version**
2. **Discipline and specific question**
3. **Relevant passage, artifact, dataset, or engineering assumption**
4. **Method and sources**
5. **Observation** — what is directly present in the source or data
6. **Interpretation or analogy** — what the contributor infers
7. **Evidence supporting the proposal**
8. **Counterevidence and alternative explanations**
9. **Limitations and uncertainty**
10. **What would weaken or falsify the proposal**
11. **References and access dates**
12. **Recommended next test**

## Research integrity rules

- Separate observation, analogy, hypothesis, interpretation, and established fact.
- Do not treat English wordplay as Hebrew etymology.
- Distinguish the KJV's English wording from Hebrew source-language analysis and from other ancient textual traditions.
- Do not treat a technical analogy as evidence that an ancient structure existed.
- Do not treat an AI-generated diagram or reconstruction as archaeological evidence.
- Record disagreements and negative findings rather than hiding them.
- Do not imply institutional endorsement, certification, employment, compensation, or formal peer review unless separately and explicitly established.
- A specialist's assessment applies to the scope of their expertise; it is not approval of the entire GEI framework.

## Interdisciplinary roundtable format

A suggested first meeting is 80 minutes:

- 10 minutes — purpose and central question
- 10 minutes — language and textual observations
- 15 minutes — hydraulic and structural model
- 15 minutes — archaeological and mechanical comparisons
- 15 minutes — objections and alternative explanations
- 10 minutes — tests and missing evidence
- 5 minutes — actions and owners

The central question for every participant:

> What would have to be true for this interpretation to be wrong?

## Secure portal requirements — future implementation

The public website is an informational and recruitment page. Do not represent sign-in, account creation, application submission, or private research workspaces as operational until they are implemented and tested.

A future portal should include:

- Authenticated server-side access control
- Roles: visitor, member, reviewer, admin
- New accounts default to member
- Reviewer/admin permissions granted explicitly and enforced server-side
- Private application and submission records
- Private contact details and consent records
- Audit trail for status changes and publication decisions
- Separate public research library for approved, intentionally published material
- Export and deletion process for personal data
- Spam protection, validation, rate limits, and secure secrets management

Never trust a client-side role field to authorize access. Do not expose private applications or drafts through static files, public GitHub issues, or browser-only storage.

## Current implementation status

The public page currently describes the research pathways and proposed participation process. Private application intake, authentication, reviewer permissions, and researcher submissions remain future implementation work and must not be described as live functionality until verified.
